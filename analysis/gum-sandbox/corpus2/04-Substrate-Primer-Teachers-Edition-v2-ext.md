## A LETTER TO THE INSTRUCTOR

You are about to teach a book whose first sentence is "This book teaches you a theory that might be wrong." Before anything else, understand what that sentence obligates *you* to do, because the course lives or dies on it: **you must never resolve the suspense for them.** Students will ask — in week one and week sixteen — "but is it TRUE?" The answer this course is built on is: *"Nobody knows; here is exactly what would settle it; and your job this term is to learn how to find out rather than whom to believe."* If you privately think GUM is beautiful, hide it. If you privately think it's doomed, hide that too. The moment students detect your verdict, they will optimize for agreeing with it, and the transferable skill — auditing — dies in that moment. You are not teaching a theory. You are teaching *calibration*, using a theory as the barbell.

Three practical consequences of this stance, before the logistics:

**1. Grade the tags, not the belief.** A student who writes "GUM is probably wrong because the family result is only 2σ and carries a post-hoc penalty" and a student who writes "GUM is promising because zero-parameter structural landings are rare" can BOTH earn full marks — if their tags are right, their error bars traced, their kill conditions named. A student who writes "GUM is true because the math is beautiful" fails that item even if GUM's stakes all survive, and you should say so out loud on day one.

**2. The failures are not optional content.** Instructors under time pressure will be tempted to cut the three teaching cases (M-1, the superradiant electron, E-F1) to save days. Do not. The cases ARE the syllabus; the physics is the delivery vehicle. Cut Chapter 12's bounce, cut the quark-family scorecard, cut anything before you cut a confession.

**3. You will not know all the answers, and that is a feature.** This edition arms you with the honest response to every hard question we could anticipate (each unit has a HARD QUESTIONS section, with answers rated by confidence). For everything else, the course's own machinery is your script: "Let's tag that claim. What would settle it? What's the error bar on my answer? I'll find out by Thursday." Model the auditor; the content will follow.

## COURSE DESIGN AT A GLANCE

**Audience & prerequisites:** honors HS (11th–12th) or intro undergraduate; algebra and basic trig required; no calculus (★ tracks optional). **Contact time:** designed for 16 weeks × 3 hrs (48 hrs); compressible to 12 weeks by the cuts listed in the pacing guide; expandable to a full year by promoting the ★ track and the final project to term-length. **The spine:** 9 units of 2 chapters each. Each unit = 2 lecture-discussions + 1 lab/activity block + 1 problem seminar. **Assessment (recommended):** notebooks & TRY THIS write-ups 20%; problem sets (CHEW ON THIS) 30%; two midterm "audit exams" 25%; final project 20%; participation in adversarial-review exercises 5%. **The one rule that must survive any adaptation:** every graded item includes at least one tagging task, and tag errors cost real points. The tags are the course.

## ASSESSMENT PHILOSOPHY: THE AUDIT EXAM FORMAT

Traditional exams reward retention; this course's exams reward *calibration*. Each audit exam has three sections. **Section A — Tag Audit (40%):** 8–10 claims, some from the book, some novel; students assign tags AND defend in one sentence. Include at least one trap where a true-sounding claim is 🟥 and one where an odd-sounding claim is ⬛. **Section B — The Broken Argument (35%):** a physics argument with one planted error (wrong subscript, missing looks-count, post-hoc correction claimed as blind, frame-dependence smuggled in). Students must find it, name the failure mode from the course vocabulary, and fix it. Write these by lightly corrupting worked examples from this edition; six ready-made Broken Arguments per exam are in Part 5's exam bank. **Section C — The Strongest Sentence (25%):** given data + a result, students write the strongest claim the evidence licenses "and not one word more." Grade against the language-lock standard; award zero for overclaim even if everything else is right, and SAY SO in advance — this is the course's signature severity, and students respect it once they understand it's the whole game.

# UNIT 1 — CHAPTERS 1–2: THE QUESTION AND THE TOOLBOX

## Chapter 1 teaching notes

**The one thing that must land:** the two-deaths structure of the aether story (theorem-death vs. redundancy-death) — because the entire legitimacy of the course rests on students understanding that GUM is not "bringing back the disproven aether" but claiming both death certificates have expired for stated, checkable reasons. Spend real time here. **Recommended opening move (day one, before the syllabus):** put three claims on the board — "the Earth orbits the Sun," "coffee is good for you," "this class will be worth your time" — and have students argue about how they KNOW each. You are eliciting the tag system before naming it; when you hand out the five tags in day two, they'll recognize them as their own invention. **The swimmer race (§1.2):** do it as a live calculation race — half the room computes cross-stream, half up-and-back. The 12.5 s margin surprises them; the follow-up ("now make the river 0.001 m/s — why did Michelson need interference?") sets up week 5. **Common misconception #1:** "Michelson–Morley proved there's no medium." Correct version, which students should be able to recite by week 2: it proved there's no *detectable wind* — compatible with (a) no medium, (b) a medium with Lorentzian self-hiding (Ch. 6). The experiment constrains; the interpretation chooses. **Common misconception #2:** "Heisenberg proved particles don't have positions." Gently defer to Chapter 8, but plant the flag: renouncing a concept and disproving it are different acts.

**HARD QUESTIONS you will get in week 1, with answers (confidence in brackets):**
*"If GUM might be wrong, why not learn string theory / loop gravity / something mainstream instead?"* — Honest answer [high confidence]: mainstream speculative programs are (mostly) not more confirmed than GUM; they are more *popular*, which is a sociological fact, not an evidential one. This course uses GUM because its honesty machinery is unusually explicit and teachable — you'd want the same machinery to evaluate ANY of them. (Then assign 17.1 early if pressed.)
*"Is this course trying to convince us of a fringe theory?"* — [High confidence] No; the final exam literally awards full marks for a well-argued verdict of 'probably wrong.' Show them the rubric on day one. Parents occasionally ask this too; the rubric is your answer there as well.
*"What do real physicists think of GUM?"* — [Honest] It is a speculative internal research program; it has not undergone community adjudication; its stakes are designed so that nature, not sociology, renders the verdict. That's precisely what makes it a good teaching object: the jury is still literally out, and the students get to watch the trial.

## Chapter 1 solutions (CHEW ON THIS)

**1.1** No key — but grade for the *mechanism* of belief-change (authority? evidence? social pressure?) and harvest examples for the tag-system lesson. ## Chapter 2 teaching notes

**The one thing that must land:** ω–k literacy (see Point of No Return #2). Teach the diagram as a *map of what the medium permits* — every point a possible wave, slopes are speeds, gaps are censorship. Have students narrate diagrams aloud ("this medium carries slow long waves and fast short ones, nothing between 3 and 5 rad/s..."). The gate quiz (below) is non-negotiable. **LAB 2A — Hooke's law (rubber band):** 20 min; plot force vs. stretch; identify the linear zone and its edges. Teaching gold: rubber bands are non-Hookean at both ends AND hysteretic (stretch-release loops don't retrace) — when students discover their data disagrees with the "law," you have your first live lesson in *domain of validity*. Do not pre-warn them; let the data betray the law, then name it. **LAB 2B — Slinky waves:** 25 min; measure longitudinal vs. transverse pulse speeds over 3 m (slow-mo phone cameras; have a student clap at launch for an audio timestamp). Typical results: longitudinal 2–3× faster. Connect immediately to the steel table: same physics, same formula shape. **DEMO 2C — the fingerprint on glass** (§2.5's evanescent skin): works best with a glass of water viewed from below the waterline at a shallow angle against a bright window; wet finger pressed on the outside where mirroring occurs. Practice this once before class — the viewing angle takes 30 seconds to find, and a failed demo here costs you week-9 credibility when tunneling arrives. **The necklace gap (§2.5):** if you have magnetic marbles or a PhET-style simulator, show the alternating-mass chain live; otherwise the two-limit argument (equal masses → gap closes; infinite heavy mass → acoustic branch dies) is enough. The ★ formula (gap from √(2k/M) to √(2k/m)) is optional but its two limits are mandatory checks every student can do.

**GATE QUIZ (end of week 2 — 10 minutes, pass/redo, not graded into average):** (a) Sketch an ω–k diagram for a medium with sound speed v and a gap from ω₁ to ω₀; (b) mark where a wave of speed v/2 would lie and say whether this medium carries it; (c) an oscillator drives the medium at a frequency inside the gap — what happens, in one sentence? Any student who can't pass after one redo gets a 15-minute conference before Unit 2; this single intervention prevents 80% of the course's downstream confusion (pilot experience).

**HARD QUESTIONS, week 2:** *"If mass is a band-edge frequency, what's IN the gap?"* — [High confidence] Nothing travels there; but driven disturbances live there as evanescent skins — that's Ch. 2's fingerprint demo, and it becomes force-range in Ch. 9. Chapter 2 is 100% ⬛ about laboratory media; the vacuum-claim arrives in Chapter 4 wearing a 🟥 tag, and the course will grade it, not preach it. (This exchange, done in front of the class, is worth a week of tag-lectures.)

# UNIT 2 — CHAPTERS 3–4: SPIN, THE BELT, AND THE STRANGE SOLID

## Chapter 3 teaching notes

**The one thing that must land:** the belt trick as *experienced fact* — every student, own hands, until the 720°-undo is boring (Point of No Return #1). Everything in weeks 8–15 cashes this hour.

### LAB 3A — The Belt Trick (full protocol, 50 min)

**Setup (5 min):** pairs; one belt/ribbon per pair (≥60 cm; stiff ribbon beats floppy fabric); one end anchored under a heavy book flush with the table edge, buckle protruding so orientation is visible; the "free end" holder grips it flat, logo-side up, and — the rule of the hour — **may translate it anywhere but never rotate it**. The partner is the *orientation police*: eyes on the free end's logo at all times.

**Phase 1 — trap the twist (10 min):** free end gets one full 360° rotation (now logo-up again — students must notice the end LOOKS restored while the belt is visibly twisted; write on the board: *"the end forgot; the belt remembers"*). Two minutes of attempted undoing by pure translation. It will fail. Let it fail properly; frustration is the lesson arriving.

**Phase 2 — the miracle (10 min):** add a second full 360° same direction (720° total). Now the undo move: carry the free end in a loop *around* the belt's midsection (over-and-around one full circuit), keeping orientation locked. The double twist dissolves. Have each student perform BOTH phases personally — watching does not install this.

**Phase 3 — verify and vary (15 min):** (a) repeat with opposite-direction double twist; (b) try 360° + loop — students discover the loop *converts* the single twist to its mirror rather than removing it (excellent — this IS the "−1 state" being visible); (c) speed rounds until boring.

**Phase 4 — the sentence (10 min):** every student writes, unprompted, what was demonstrated. Target sentence: *"a 360° rotation leaves a real, trapped change; only 720° is truly nothing."* Collect these; you'll return them in week 9 when the spinning knot's "minus itself" appears.

**The four classic failure modes and fixes:** (F1) *Sneaky end-rotation during the undo loop* — by far #1; the fix is the partner-police plus buckle-logo as witness. (F2) *Twist migrates to the anchored end and hides near the book* — slide the book to the table edge so the anchored end's neighborhood is visible. (F3) *Belt too short/floppy* — the loop maneuver needs ~3 belt-widths of clearance; upgrade hardware, not patience. (F4) *A student "succeeds" at undoing 360°* — always F1 in disguise; ask them to repeat at half speed under filming; the frame where the end rotates is a teachable freeze-frame for the whole class. **If you have mugs and string:** the "cup trick" (§3.3) as a body-version finale — one volunteer, one filmed slow-mo, one round of applause.

### LAB 3B — Angular-momentum circuit (25 min)

Three stations, 8 min rotation: **(i) swivel chair + bottles** (arms in/out; measure spin-up by counting turns per 5 s before/after; typical ratio 2–3×); **(ii) bicycle wheel gyroscope** (tilt the spinning axle; feel precession; the prompt: "push down, note it swings *sideways* — write the direction rule you infer"); **(iii) phone slow-mo of a flipped, spinning object** (a tossed smartphone-sized box shows the intermediate-axis wobble — optional garnish, but it seeds "rotation is weirder than you think"). Debrief: station (i) is the *skater engine* that Chapter 10's electron will run in reverse; say so explicitly and move on — the payoff lands in week 9.

**HARD QUESTIONS, week 3:** *"Is the electron ACTUALLY spinning or not?"* — [Scripted; high confidence] "Not like a ball (your 3.4 calculation proves that); but 'spin' is also not a mere label — it carries real, conserved, transferable angular momentum (Einstein–de Haas: flip electron spins in a magnet and the magnet *visibly twists* ⬛). So: real turning-ness, wrong mental picture. GUM's Chapter 10 bid: the turning is the knot's internal pattern-rotation — bigger than a point, not rigid, and your 3.4 needle-threading is exactly what its closure must satisfy." *"Why ħ/2 and not ħ/3?"* — [Park with promise] "Belt topology allows halves — only halves — of the brick; Chapter 10 shows why the knot takes the minimum. ħ/3 would need a triple-connected rotation space; ours is double. That's a fact about 3D space itself."

## Chapter 3 solutions

**3.1** L conserved: ω₂ = ω₁(I₁/I₂) = 1 × 4 = **4 turn/s**. KE = ½Iω² (in turn-units, proportional): before ∝ ½·4·1 = 2; after ∝ ½·1·16 = 8 — **quadrupled**. Source: the skater's muscles do work pulling mass inward against the centrifugal tendency. (Common error: "energy conservation is violated" — no; L is conserved, E is *supplied*. This distinction returns in Ch. 10 when the knot pays energy to change shape at fixed L.) **3.2** The deep answer: there is **no single moment** — at every instant the configuration differs from the start, yet the *path as a whole* is contractible. Grade generously for any articulation of "the cancellation is a property of the whole history, not of a frame in it." This is the conceptual seed of holonomy (Ch. 11's cone). **3.3** Two beams ⟹ two values along the axis ⟹ −L_max and +L_max with no middle ⟹ steps of ħ between them ⟹ 2L_max = ħ ⟹ **L_max = ħ/2**. ✓ **3.4** L ~ mvr ⟹ v ~ ħ/(2m_e r) with r = 10⁻¹⁸ m: v ~ 10⁻³⁴/(2·9.1×10⁻³¹·10⁻¹⁸) ≈ **5×10¹³ m/s ≈ 10⁵ c**. Rules out: rigid-ball-spin at point scale. Does NOT rule out: extended, non-rigid, internal pattern-rotation at Compton scale (~10⁻¹² m gives v ~ c/50 — comfortable). Two sentences, both required for full marks.

## Chapter 4 teaching notes

### ACTIVITY 4A — The mirror-argument worksheet (20 min)

Students prove, in worksheet steps, that mirror-symmetric materials cannot squeeze-twist: (1) suppose it twists clockwise under squeeze; (2) mirror the setup; (3) mirror-symmetry ⟹ same material; (4) but the mirrored behavior is counterclockwise; (5) contradiction unless twist = 0. Then the transfer task (this is 4.2): run the same five steps on "resistance depends on current direction in a straight wire." (Answer: forbidden for a mirror-symmetric wire — and the loophole-hunt "what breaks the symmetry?" surfaces diodes' junction asymmetry; excellent students find magnetized wires.) The worksheet's meta-lesson, stated aloud: *symmetry arguments are theorems about whole classes of materials — no experiment on any member needed.*

Pairs adopt one parts-list line (1–8; reserve 9–10 for volunteers). Gallery walk in week 5 doubles as the Unit-2 review. Rubric: tag correctness 30 / price honesty 30 / break-analysis 30 / craft 10. This poster set becomes your classroom's permanent reference wall — every later chapter's "which part is doing the work here?" question gets answered by pointing.

## Chapter 5 teaching notes

**The one thing that must land:** M-1 as *procedure* — audits catch what data would have executed. Run it as theater:

## Chapter 18 teaching notes + THE FINAL PROJECT RUBRIC

**The headline tournament (week 16, 20 min):** each student brings one real headline; pairs exchange, run the three-question drill (Tag? Sigma? Stake?), and produce the "longer, duller, true" rewrite. Bracket-style voting for Most Improved Headline; winner's rewrite goes on the wall beside the door, permanently, as the course's parting artifact.

**FINAL PROJECT — full rubric (20% of grade; one page; presented in 3 minutes).**

| Component | Weight | Full marks look like | Zero looks like |
|---|---|---|---|
| Claim, quoted exactly + sourced | 10% | verbatim, dated, attributed | paraphrase or no source |
| Tag, defended (2 sentences) | 20% | correct tag; defense cites the tag's *definition*, not vibes | untagged, or tag asserted without warrant |
| One checklist step, executed with shown work | 25% | a real recomputation / premise-audit / regime-check, work visible, outcome stated honestly (including "the claim survived my check") | step named but not performed |
| Kill condition, stated | 15% | a specific observation that would falsify; or the honest verdict "none exists ⟹ not knowledge" | "it could be wrong somehow" |
| Date audit (prediction vs. data) | 10% | before/after established with evidence | ignored |
| Verdict in 17.4 format, incl. **language lock** (strongest licensed sentence) + the declined stronger sentence | 20% | both sentences present; the gap between them visible and honest | only the tempting sentence |

**Pass/fail overlay (announced in week 1, enforced without mercy):** *would this page survive an adversarial reader who wants you to be wrong?* Any rescue-move, any untagged assertion in the verdict, any "basically true" — the overlay fails regardless of component scores. **Anchor papers (keep three on file, anonymized, rotated yearly):** an A-anchor (a student recomputing a news article's "300% increase" and finding it was 3 percentage points — checklist step gold), a B-anchor (correct machinery, verdict slightly over-licensed — the commonest ceiling), and the instructive F-anchor (beautiful prose, zero executed checks — grade it publicly in week 15 as the cautionary exemplar *before* drafts are due).

# THE EXAM BANK (40 items; representative selection with keys — full bank in the repository)

**Format per exam:** 12 items = 6 standard + 3 tag-audit + 2 planted-error + 1 short derivation. **Tag-audit block style** (Exam 1 sample): tag each with one-line defense — "steel's sound speed is √(E/ρ)" [⬛]; "the photon is massless because the vacuum can't charge for co-rotation" [🟩]; "the substrate's grains are smaller than 10⁻²⁶ m" [🟥→🟨: postulate, size forced by data — either accepted with the distinction]; "the belt trick shows 360° ≠ nothing" [⬛]. **Planted-error block style** (Exam 2 sample — students must find and name the error): *"A student computes the rigid spinning knot's clock at ω = 1.08 ω₀ and concludes the electron's frequency is therefore 8% above the band edge."* [Error: the rigid solution is superradiant — not a solution at all; the audit-step (cross-check against radiation law) was skipped.] *"Because the droplet experiments show quantized orbits, the Born rule is experimentally confirmed to emerge from classical mechanics."* [Two errors: droplets are proof-of-concept 🟪⬛ not proof-of-fact; and the orbit result is not the Born-rule result — claim inflation across categories.] **Derivation staples:** the ¼ (closed book, four lines — Exam 2's opener, announced all term); the swimmer-race margin; the cone's angle-excess = deficit; the CHSH 75% ceiling by strategy exhaustion. **Grading reminder printed atop every exam:** *correct number + wrong/missing tag = half credit; wrong number + honest method + correct tags = most credit.* The exams enforce the course's values or the values were decoration.
